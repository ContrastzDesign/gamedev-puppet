Googly-Eye Shorts Style Guide

The target look for our vertical explainers: a toy-like toon-3D world with ink outlines and blue shadows. Real household objects with stuck-on googly eyes are the cast, and colour explains what's happening. Every rule here comes from frames of the reference videos. Click any frame to see it larger.

At a glance

The six rules

  • One toy world per videoA single continuous set: a lime field under a blue sky, a workshop desk, or a cleanroom. Nothing is a flat backdrop.
  • Toon shading with ink outlinesTwo or three flat tones per surface, a hard terminator, and thin dark outlines of even width. No gradients on objects.
  • Shadows are blueCast shadows are hard-edged and saturated blue #3d518c, never a darker version of the ground.
  • Real objects at real proportionsChunky and simplified, but true to the real thing. Googly eyes are the only cartoon addition.
  • Colour is the explanationLime marks the part being named, red marks the problem, and a mint tick marks "solved". Nothing else uses these colours.
  • The camera never parksSlow orbits, push-ins through see-through walls, pull-backs to wide shots. Cuts are rare, and the last frame matches the first.

Rendering

Shading, outlines, shadows, texture

These are native-resolution crops (640 × 640 pixels from the 1080 × 1920 frames), so line weights and textures are shown at their real size.

ElementRule
ShadingToon ramp with 2–3 flat steps and a hard edge between them. The shadow step is only about 15–25% darker than the lit step, so colours stay bright. No specular highlights, except a single soft band on glass and chrome.
OutlinesSilhouette plus major creases, about 2–3 px at 1080 wide. Near-black #1c1c20, or dark brown on wood. Even weight that doesn't taper. Small parts keep the same pixel width, so they look relatively bolder.
Cast shadowsOutdoors: one sun, hard edges, solid #3d518c at near-full opacity. Indoors: a tone about 10% darker plus diagonal hatch lines.
LightingOne warm-white key light from high front-left, plus a bright sky fill so nothing goes dark. Contact shadows are present but ambient occlusion is minimal.
TextureVery light: faint paper noise on big flat areas, hatch lines on metal trim, ink grain strokes on wood. Never photographic textures.
Glass and transparent thingsPale blue #b9d6ee-ish at 40–60% opacity, keeping the outline and one or two lighter ring bands. X-ray walls use the same treatment.
AtmosphereA slight haze toward the horizon. No depth-of-field blur, no bloom, no lens effects. Everything stays crisp at phone size.

Modelling

Objects and props

  • True proportions, simplified detail. Keep the real silhouette and two or three signature details (brass corners, hex nuts, screw heads, fins), then stop. Chamfer or round every edge slightly.
  • Materials read by colour, not texture. Wood is brown with ink grain, metal is flat grey with one highlight band, glass is pale blue and translucent.
  • Hero object size: in medium shots the hero fills about 40–60% of the frame width and sits just below centre.
  • Cutaways are clean slices with flat interior colours (the eyeball shows a red, white and blue section), not gore or realism.
  • Analogy props live in the same world and use the same render (a car, a hand, a burger, a brick, a school bus). They never appear as photos or 2D clip art.
  • Labels are 3D placards: small white plates with black caps text attached to the object ("PINHOLE", "MOUNT", "×1,000,000").

Characters

Googly-eye household objects

The source uses three dominoes, each with two stuck-on googly eyes. They're the thing the explanation acts on: the scene the camera photographs. We apply the same treatment to household items. The object stays real, the eyes are the only addition, and the pupils do the acting.

Character rules

  1. The object stays the object. Keep the real shape, real proportions and real details. Add two googly eyes and nothing else: no limbs, no drawn mouth, no eyebrows, no cartoon head.
  2. Its own features make the face. A domino's divider is a mouth line, a mug handle is an ear, a sponge's scrub pad is hair. Give at most one costume accessory (a bow tie) per cast.
  3. Eyes sit on the front face, centred left-to-right, about a quarter of the way down. On short, wide objects, put them on the main front surface (the sponge's yellow body, not the green pad).
  4. Pupils act. Each pupil moves on its own springy lag, overshooting and settling in about 0.4 s when the body moves. Point both at the subject: the part flashing lime, the problem, or the camera on reaction beats. No blinks; googly eyes can't blink.
  5. Bodies act as whole objects: pop or slide in with a dust puff, a small squash on landing, lean toward the action, tilt back on surprise, and huddle together.
  6. Cast in threes. Use the same type of object in three colours (cream, coral, saffron) or three related objects from one room. Keep the cast's colours away from the signal colours.
  7. They appear inside the explanation too. They show on screens, in projected images and in photos (the dominoes on the camera's back wall and on the phone screen), which ties the cast to the topic.

Visual language

Signal language

These devices repeat in every video, so the viewer learns them in the first 20 seconds. Use them with the same meaning every time.

Lime: "this part"

Tint the whole named part lime and keep its toon shading. Hold it, then fade back to its own colour.

Camera 0:35 · hold about 0.5–1 s

Lime on a whole system

Big assemblies flash lime too. The outline stays and the inner detail stays readable.

Live sample, drawn to scale on a 9:16 frame with the closest free font.

Caption bands: top at 10–17% down, lower at 65–73% down. Use one or the other, never both at once.

Shorts interface (approximate): the bottom strip and the right-hand button column. Keep captions and key action out of these.

FontA heavy geometric sans with a single-storey g and a slanted t cut. The closest free match is Montserrat ExtraBold 800, tracking −1%.
SizeAbout 76–80 px on a 1080-wide frame (7.2% of width). Long captions run up to 85% of the width on one line.
CaseLowercase, except units and proper nouns ("608 mm²", "EUV").
ColourInk #1c1c20; problem words in red #e8341f.
GlowA soft white outer glow, about 8–12 px at 50–70%. No box, no hard stroke, no drop shadow.
PlacementWhichever band the hero object isn't in. One caption at a time.
MotionReveal left to right with a per-letter blur-and-fade over about 0.3 s, and exit the same way. No bounce, scale or pop.

Camera

Camera and framing

  • Always moving. Use slow orbits around the hero, push-ins (often through a wall that turns see-through), and pull-backs to a wide shot to re-establish. The camera stops only on a "freeze".
  • Three distances on rotation: wide (hero small in the world, sky visible), medium (hero 40–60% of width), inside or close (a part fills the frame). Return to wide after each big reveal.
  • Lens: a moderate perspective with no fisheye. An estimated 35–40° vertical field of view is a good starting point; judge by eye against the frames below.
  • Horizon sits in the upper half on medium shots, so the field fills the lower frame and the blue shadows read.
  • Cuts are rare. Use a hard cut only for a big jump (a new angle or a cutaway), and a fade to white only for a change of world.
  • Loop: the last frame matches the first. Clear the stage with a sparkle dissolve back to the opening composition.

Checklist

Do / don't

Do

  • Keep one continuous world per video and move the camera through it.
  • Paint every cast shadow blue and keep it hard-edged.
  • Keep ink outlines on everything, including glass and x-ray walls.
  • Flash the named part lime, and only that part.
  • Keep objects at real proportions, with googly eyes as the only addition.
  • Aim the pupils at whatever matters in the shot.
  • Keep captions in a band the hero isn't in, clear of the Shorts interface.
  • End on the opening frame.

Don't

  • Use flat backdrops, photo textures or realistic materials.
  • Use bloom, depth-of-field blur, lens flares or glow on light effects.
  • Give objects arms, legs, mouths, eyebrows or blinks.
  • Use lime, problem red or mint as ordinary prop colours.
  • Put boxes or thick strokes behind captions, or bounce them in.
  • Use 2D title stamps or flat cel shading. That's the channel's older look (the solar video), which it has since dropped.
  • Cut between locations when a camera move can get there.
  • Hold a static camera, except on a deliberate freeze.

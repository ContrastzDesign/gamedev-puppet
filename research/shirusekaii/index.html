Summary

The short version

  1. One object, built from nothing, one fix at a time. Each beat is a problem, then a fix, then "That's the part." There are 7–11 beats per video.
  2. One toy-like 3D world. Toon shading, thin ink outlines, flat lime grass, a gradient sky, and hard shadows painted saturated blue.
  3. Colour does the explaining. Lime marks the part being named. Red marks what's wrong. A green tick marks "solved".
  4. Captions are keywords, not subtitles. 1–4 words, heavy rounded type, black, with problem words in red.
  5. The camera keeps moving and the video loops. Long continuous moves with few cuts. The last frame matches the first.
  6. Fast, plain narration. 176–209 words per minute, second person, present tense. No intro and no sign-off.
  7. The characters are objects with googly eyes. In the newest video, three dominoes with stuck-on eyes are what the camera photographs. They're the subject of the scene, never presenters.

Writing

The script formula: build it from scratch

Each video invents the object from its simplest form. Every new part is a fix for the problem the last part created. The viewer never gets a list of facts. They watch a chain of decisions, so each part arrives already explained.

"If you put X… you've got a Y."→ "But…" problem→ "So you…" fix→ "That's the [part]."→ repeat 7–11× → callback / twist→ loop to frame 1

Rendering

The look

Stylised 3D that reads like an illustrated textbook. Objects keep realistic proportions and recognisable detail (screws, brass corners, threads, fins). They're simplified into chunky, slightly rounded shapes rather than made cute.

  • Shading: toon, with one base tone and one shadow tone per surface and a hard terminator. A faint cross-hatch or paper texture sits on surfaces, heavier on the indoor set.
  • Outlines: thin, dark ink lines (about 2–3 px at 1080 wide) on silhouettes and major creases. They stay uniform in width.
  • Cast shadows are hard-edged and painted a saturated blue (#3d518c), never a darker green. This single choice does most of the "illustrated" work.
  • Sky: a vertical gradient from mid-blue to pale haze at the horizon. Flat grey-blue mountain silhouettes sit along a thin black horizon line. Clouds are puffy two-tone shapes, white on top and grey underneath.
  • Ground: a flat, saturated lime-green field with outlined, spiky dark-green grass tufts and small round clover bushes. The trees are cartoon lollipop shapes.
  • Indoor variant (chip video): mint wall, beige pegboard with tools, a wooden desk with a blue cutting mat, a lamp, a monitor and a red mug. The palette is more pastel, the hatching heavier, and the cleanroom is near-white.
  • No depth-of-field blur and no bloom. Everything stays crisp, so the outlines stay readable at phone size.

Characters

Googly-eye object characters

Only the camera video has them. Three domino tiles (saffron, cream with a black bow tie, coral-red) stand in the field as the scene being photographed. You see them upside down on the box's back wall, on film, on the sensor and on the phone screen. In the last 15 seconds they crowd in as big foreground characters around the phone.

Anatomy, measured from the 1:45.7 crop

  • Cast as a set of three. Same object, three colours (saffron, cream, coral). One of them gets a single accessory (the bow tie), which is enough to tell them apart.
  • Acting: they slide or pop in, lean toward the action, and tilt. The pupils do the rest. There are no blinks, mouths or limbs.
  • Their job is to be what the explanation acts on: the photographed scene. They give the viewer someone to root for without stopping the explanation.
  • The other three videos have no characters at all. The solar video's fridge is a plain prop that gets a green tick. Googly eyes are new for this channel, so leaning into them puts us ahead of the format rather than copying a settled template.

Camera and editing

Camera, cuts and the loop

  • One virtual camera that keeps moving. Slow orbits around the hero object, push-ins (often straight through a wall that turns see-through), then a pull-back to a wide shot to re-establish before the next problem. It almost never holds still, except on a deliberate "freeze".
  • Hard cuts are rare and only for big jumps. The turbine video has five (0:24, 1:28, 1:39, 1:49, 1:54). The chip video has one fade to white into the cleanroom (about 1:06). In the camera video, the only scene-change hits were a burst at 1:31, during the RGB-filter flash.
  • Something new every 1–2 seconds: a prop, a highlight, an arrow or a caption change. The camera move covers the gaps.
  • Openings are instant: there's motion in the first 0.3 s. The camera box drops onto the tripod with dust; the turbine explodes; the GPU assembles.
  • Seamless loop. The last frame matches the first: empty tripod to empty tripod; turbine shutdown back to the explosion; the GPU exploding apart as at the start. On Shorts this makes the replay feel like part of the video.

On-screen text

Captions

WhatA 1–4 word keyword summary of the line, not a transcript. Lowercase except proper nouns and units: twist, gearbox, photos, EUV lithography, taste ≠ recipe.
ProblemsThe problem words are red: upside down too dim blur one distance only it turns white too much light heads cut off! clack! long lever fence boards too close messy air one wrong wire expensive sand
TypeA heavy, rounded geometric sans in near-black, with no box or thick stroke. Some frames show a faint light halo for contrast. Large: a 3-word caption spans about 60–75% of the frame width.
PositionEither the top band (about 10–16% down) or the lower-middle (about 65–72% down). Placement moves to wherever the hero object isn't, and only one caption shows at a time.
MotionReveals left to right with a soft per-letter blur-and-fade over about 0.3 s, and leaves the same way. No bounce or pop.
RhythmA new caption every 2–4 s, timed to the key noun in the narration.
ExtrasThe chip video ends on follow for more. It's the only call to action in the four videos.

Sound

Voice and audio

  • One narrator, calm and quick, at 176–209 words per minute. That's fast for explainers, and the newest video is the fastest.
  • Pauses are tiny. In each video, at most one gap longer than 0.4 s drops below −45 dB. Sentences run straight into each other.
  • Loudness: −17 LUFS integrated with a loudness range of only 1.9–3.0 LU, measured on all four as YouTube serves them. That's a heavily levelled voice.
  • Music is faint or absent. YouTube's auto-captions tag "[music]" in places, but in the spectrogram the gaps between phrases go almost silent, so there's no steady bed under the voice.
  • The script names its own sounds ("That clack you hear", "you hear that whoosh") and the caption shows them (clack!, whoosh). I couldn't confirm from the waveform whether a sound effect plays under them.

For our videos

Making it ours: household items with googly eyes

The domino idea generalises cleanly. Take an ordinary object, render it in the same toon style at its real proportions, and stick two craft googly eyes on it. Here are the rules I'd carry over, based on what the dominoes do:

  1. The object stays a real object. Same shape, same details. The eyes are the only addition: no limbs, no mouth, no cartoon head.
  2. Use the object's own features as the face. A mug's handle reads as an ear and a toaster's slots as eyebrows. Steam, a label or a sticker can be an accessory, like the domino's divider line and bow tie.
  3. Eyes: about 0.29 × the object's width each, 0.42 × width apart, sitting in the upper quarter of the front face. Use an off-white dome, a thin ink ring, a grey lower crescent and a flat black pupil half the eye's size.
  4. Pupils do the acting. Each pupil is on its own springy lag, overshooting when the object moves and settling in about 0.4 s. They aim at whatever the narration is about: the lightning, the shadow, the part flashing lime.
  5. Body acting only: pop or slide in with a dust puff, a small squash on landing, a lean toward the action, and a tilt back on "surprise". No blinks.
  6. Cast of three per video, the same kind of object in three colours plus one accessory. Use the palette's saffron, cream and coral, or pick the topic's own colours.
  7. Their job is to be what the explanation acts on. They're what gets photographed, powered, cooled or cleaned. They react, but they never narrate or explain.
